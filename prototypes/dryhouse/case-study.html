Solar Dry House: when will it be dry?

A web and mobile app for people who run a solar dryer. It reads the live weather where the dryer stands and turns it into the one answer they need: how many days until the chilli, turmeric or copra on the trays is dry, and on which date.

  • StackHTML, CSS, JavaScript, Open-Meteo, Wikimedia Commons

The problem

The dryer is faster than the open sun, but the timing is still a guess

A solar dry house is a polycarbonate greenhouse with a black floor and a fan. It runs 15–25 °C hotter than the air outside, so chilli that takes two weeks on a tarpaulin dries in five days, clean and out of the rain. What it doesn't do is tell anyone when to load, when to open the vents, or when a batch is finished.

“How many days?”

It depends on the crop, the slice, and this week's sun and humidity. A cloudy spell can add days that nobody planned for.

“Is it too hot in there?”

At noon the house passes 60 °C. That's fine for copra, but chilli turns brown and moringa loses its colour and value.

“How dry is dry?”

A low target like 3% moisture sounds simple. Leaves can get there; mango and salted fish never will in a solar dryer, however long they stay.

Design goals

What the app had to get right

  • Answer with a date, not a moisture model.
  • Use the weather where the dryer is, without asking anyone to read a forecast.
  • Be honest when the target is impossible rather than print a number that will never come true.
  • Let people find their crop by the name they use and the way it looks.
  • Feel calm and easy on a budget phone in bright sun, as well as on a laptop.
Key decisions

How the design answered those goals

Turn the forecast into drying days

The app takes Open-Meteo's sunshine, temperature and humidity for the next 15 days and estimates the air inside the house: the sun sets how far it climbs above the outside temperature, and heating dries the air. Each day gets a rating GoodFairSlow, and a cloudy day counts as only part of a drying day. That's how “5 sunny days” becomes ready Wed 4 Nov.

Lead with the sky, and say what to do about it

The Today card takes the colour of the weather (warm on a sunny day, grey when it's overcast, deep blue at night) and ends in a plain instruction: keep the fans running, open the vents, or keep the door shut until sunrise. The forecast row below shows the estimated temperature inside the house rather than outside, because that's the number that decides the batch.

Warn before the heat spoils a crop

Every material carries its safe air temperature. When the forecast puts the house above it, the material's page names the days and the fix: open the vents and lay shade net from 11 am to 2 pm.

Say when 3% can't be reached

The brief asked how long each material takes to reach 3% moisture. Every material levels off at a moisture set by the hot, dry air around it. That's about 2–3% for leaves, vegetables and spices, but 8–12% for sugary fruit, salted fish and tamarind. The app fits a drying curve between the fresh moisture and the safe-storage point and stops at that floor. So 24 of the 58 materials show a day count to 3%, and the rest say Can't reach 3% and give the lowest they can get to.

Show the cost of going lower

Dropping turmeric from safe storage at 10% to 3% takes about six more sunny days. The page says so, and that it's worth it for powders and long storage but costs weight, colour and aroma.

Real photos and local names

Fifty-eight materials, from curry leaves to cow dung cakes, each with a real photograph from Wikimedia Commons, credited on its page. Search works in English and in the names people actually use: manjal finds turmeric, karuvadu finds dried fish. Category chips and a 3 / 5 / 8 / 10% switch change every card's day count at once.

Plan a whole batch

The batch screen takes the fresh weight and tray area. It shows how much water has to leave, how many kilos come back, whether the load fits on the trays, and the ready date with this week's weather.

Location

The web app asks, the phone already knows

Everything depends on where the dryer is, so the first thing the app needs is a place. The two versions get it differently, because people use them differently.

  • Web: a laptop is usually not at the dryer, so the web app asks “Where is your dry house?” and searches towns as you type. Location access is offered as a button, never sprung on page load.
  • Mobile: the phone is usually standing next to the dryer. The app reads the device location as soon as it opens, names the town, and refreshes it on each launch if the phone has moved more than 3 km. If permission is refused, it falls back to the town search and says why.

Where it stands

Both prototypes on this page are the working app, with live weather. The screenshots were captured from it, with the mobile version reading a simulated phone location in Coimbatore.

The drying figures are typical guide values for greenhouse-type dryers, and the inside temperature is an estimate from the forecast, not a sensor reading. Neither has been checked against a real dryer yet. Next would be logging a few real batches to tune the model, and app-store builds if a native wrapper is needed.

What I took from it

The honest answer is part of the design

The request was a number: days to 3%. The useful product turned out to be a date, plus a clear “not possible” where the physics says no. The data is the easy part. The design work was deciding what to say when the weather, the crop or the target makes the answer uncomfortable.
